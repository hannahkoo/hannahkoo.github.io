Tooling Was Never the Engineers' Job

Old news in tech. Somehow still heresy in design.

I’m not an engineer, and building tools was never something I thought of as part of my job. But after years of working in design and localization production, I knew our workflow well enough to see where a small tool could make a difference. With AI, I was able to turn that understanding into something tangible.

What surprised me wasn’t how quickly AI could generate code. It was how much the outcome depended on knowing what needed to be built in the first place.

If you spend time in tech circles, none of this is new. AI lowers the barrier, domain knowledge is the real moat — you’ve heard it a hundred times. But in a lot of design studios it still hasn’t quite landed; the quiet assumption is that building tools is an engineer’s job, and the rest of us wait for the roadmap. I assumed that too, for longer than I’d like to admit.

AI can generate ideas. Knowing which ones matter is harder.

Early on, I gave Claude a prompt that was going around on social media: “find me a business to build. Pull the top products launched on Product Hunt in the last 30 days, break down the problems they solve, find the patterns, size the markets, rank five underserved gaps a solo builder could realistically ship.”

It was genuinely good at the gathering and organizing — I had a tidy, confident-looking map in minutes. And I couldn’t really use any of it, because I had no way to tell which of those openings was real. I could ask for market sizes all day; that didn’t give me the judgment to tell a promising market from an interesting-sounding sentence. The AI gave me an answer. I just wasn’t equipped to trust it.

The difference was context.

Building for my own team was the opposite feeling. The tool itself wasn’t clever. It took over the busywork in our localization production — the repetitive, mechanical steps between a finished design and a shipped asset across dozens of languages. I’d done all of it by hand for years, so I knew exactly where it dragged and what everyone had quietly learned to tolerate.

That’s also why I wasn’t just handing AI a vague wish and hoping for the best. I actually designed it — worked out what each button should do, what triggers what, how someone moves through it from start to finish. The interaction, the logic, the small decisions that make a tool feel obvious instead of annoying — that was the part that took real work, and it’s what my UX training was for. AI turned it into working code; it couldn’t have told me what the tool should be.

The tools will keep changing

Most weeks I can’t keep up. A new model, a new tool, a new workflow every weekend — and if I’m not constantly catching up, I feel like I’m already behind. Some weeks it’s genuinely exhausting. I work in tech and I still feel it.

But I've been through cycles of changing tools before. I learned Flash as a teenager, and over the years, I've watched the tools I started with evolve, disappear, or give way to something new.

That experience has changed how I approach new technology. I still enjoy exploring new tools, but I've learned that I don't need to master every new release to keep doing meaningful work. The tools I use will change, and so will the way I work. I'm becoming more comfortable with that.

What hasn’t expired is knowing my own corner of the work — where it hurts, what people actually need, which problems are worth solving. That part has never shown up in a changelog.

And lately I keep noticing the same thing: AI output is usually technically right. Polished, even. But a lot of it feels empty to me — correct and a little lifeless at once. Whatever makes something feel like a person made it, like someone actually cared how it felt to use — that still has to come from me.

So I’ve stopped trying to swallow all of it. I pick up the tools my work actually asks for, and I let the rest go by. On the weeks I’m tired of running to keep up, I’ve started letting myself slow down and make the thing I care about instead. The tools will keep changing. Why something’s worth building hasn’t.
