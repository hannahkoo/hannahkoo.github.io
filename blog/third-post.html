We Might Be Moving from UX to AX

The next users aren't human. And if you've ever written markdown for your AI, you've already started designing for them.

A few days ago I was rebuilding this portfolio, and somewhere in the middle I asked my AI a dumb-sounding question: how do I make this readable for the hiring agents that might screen it before a person ever does? It gave me a list — cleaner meta descriptions, structured data, a plain-text summary a machine could parse without rendering the page. I did most of it. Then I sat there for a second, because I'd just spent an afternoon designing for a user with no eyes.

We're in one of those transitional stretches that feels bigger while you're standing in it. The systems are getting smarter than us at more and more things — but the part that keeps getting missed is that smarter doesn't mean self-sufficient. An agent still can't automate or govern a domain it has no context for. It needs the shape of the work from the people who do the work. (If you read my first post, that's the thing I keep circling back to.)

So the question I can't put down is a designer's question: if more of what we build is going to be read, used, and acted on by agents instead of people — where's our job in that?

Here's the funny part. We're already doing it. We just haven't called it anything.

Every time a designer or a vibe-coder says "write it in markdown, the model handles that better" — that's it. That's design for a non-human user. Structuring a note in Obsidian so an agent can actually traverse it. Dropping an llms.txt so a crawler gets the clean version. Formatting a prompt so the thing on the other end doesn't get lost. None of that is UX. It's AX — agent experience — and it turns out it already has a name. People started using it while I wasn't looking.

So I've started thinking of it as a shift, not a replacement. UX was built to human standards — what's easy for a person to see, click, understand. AX asks a stranger set of questions. What does an agent need in order to "experience" something correctly? Where do its interactions even happen — not on a screen, but in a file, an API, a payment it makes on your behalf? What does a good experience mean for a user that doesn't feel anything?

I don't have clean answers. The far end of this — agents transacting, agents with their own economy, maybe their own small social spaces — is the deep end, and I'd be lying if I said I fully get it yet. I'm watching, not pronouncing.

But the near end I can touch. It's the afternoon I spent making my own site legible to a machine that might read it before a human does. Small, dumb, concrete — and probably the first real UX-for-agents thing I've ever shipped.

We're living in a stretch that's more cinematic than the movies were. The least I can do is notice who the audience is becoming.
